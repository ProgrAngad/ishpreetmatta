Case study: consumer segmentation & market sizing

WearPepper: the Hathor Collection

A swimwear line for a direct-to-consumer intimates brand that only makes AA, A and B cups. Most of the work was segmentation and market sizing, the part that has to happen before anyone spends the launch budget.

Challenge

WearPepper is a real brand. Jaclyn Fu and Lia Winograd started it in 2017 around one unserved fit problem: bras actually cut for AA, A and B cups, rather than a C graded down. That worked for them in bras and then in athleisure, and swimwear was the obvious next step.

Swimwear is where the fit problem gets worst. A top cut for a fuller bust gaps at the cup and drags on the strap, and a lot of the category solves that by adding padding nobody asked for. Making the product wasn't really the hard part. The question was who buys it, where they live, and whether there are enough of them to justify a $2M launch budget.

My role

Sole author. Both documents are mine: the integrated marketing communications plan and the segmentation framework sitting under it. That covers the segmentation itself, the geographic tiering, the market sizing, the competitor review, the positioning statement, and the price and margin structure.

School project, York University, 2023. WearPepper is a real company, but the Hathor Collection is my proposal for them, not something they asked for. It wasn't a client engagement and the line was never made.

Approach

Three tiers, primary through tertiary, each one profiled on the same four dimensions. That was the whole point of doing it as a grid. You can compare the tiers instead of describing each one in turn and hoping the reader holds all three in their head.

  • Geography went by climate, not population. Swimwear sells where it's warm, so the primary tier leans into the warm regions of Canada and the US. The cold-region customer who travels ended up as her own tier rather than an afterthought.
  • Sizing was top down. I started from the published swimwear and beachwear category figures and cut them to the share of women in the applicable range.
  • Only the primary tier got sized. The other two don't get their own marketing spend, so any dollar figure I put against them would have been made up to fill the cell.

The work

The segmentation framework, and the market it sizes.

Primary

The Core Fit

Women who wear AA to B cups and can't find swimwear that fits.

Geography
Warm-weather markets only. Canada Pacific coast, Prairie provinces, Central Canada US Southwest, Lower Midwest, Southeast, California, Texas
Demographics
Women, 18 to 50. Family life cycle: young single, married, family nests 1 to 3, empty nest 1. Lower-middle class and up. I left education and occupation out on purpose, they don't predict this need. About 32% of women fall in the applicable range.
Lifestyle & values
Socially active, with money to spend on it. What they wear to the beach is self-expression, not swim kit. They want a fit that flatters rather than one that adds volume.
Buying behaviour
In their own words: a top that doesn't gap at the cup and doesn't slide on the strap. They buy on Instagram, on the brand's own site, and from the stockists already carrying the bras. Decisions come out of research and word of mouth, judged on fit and a clear point of difference. Peaks in summer.

Secondary

Globetrotters & First Fit

Cold-region travellers, and adolescents buying a first swimsuit that fits.

Geography
The inverse of the primary tier. Cold regions, buying ahead of a trip. Canada including the northern territories US Mountain frontier, Upper Midwest, Northeastern highlands First Fit national, Canada and the US
Demographics
Globetrotters: women 18 to 50, same life-cycle spread as the primary tier, lower-middle class and up, well paid and educated.
First Fit: 10 to 17. In the US that's 13% of the adolescent population, 49% of them female. Canada is 15%.
Lifestyle & values
Globetrotters: money to spend, socially active, swimwear as self-expression.
First Fit: the spending follows a caregiver's income rather than their own, and it's a milestone purchase, which is how it gets treated.
Buying behaviour
Same product need and the same channels as the primary tier. Timing is what's different. They buy against a trip rather than a season, which pulls demand outside the summer peak.

Tertiary

Turtle Doves

Men buying swimwear as a gift.

Geography
National across Canada, warm regions and cold. A gift doesn't follow the weather where the buyer happens to live.
Demographics
Men, 18 to 50. Young adults, married, family nests 1 to 3, empty nest 1. Lower-middle class and up. Education and occupation don't change the decision here.
Lifestyle & values
Money to spend. The value sits with the person receiving it rather than with the product, so the messaging has a completely different job to do.
Buying behaviour
Buying for someone else, so fit reads as a risk instead of a benefit. Peaks at holidays and celebrations rather than in summer. The only tier whose calendar runs opposite the category.

Three tiers, three different calendars: summer, travel, gifting. That's the bit that turned a description of customers into an actual promotional calendar.

Outcome

It was a school project, so the line never got made and there's no sell-through to show. What it did produce is the set of decisions a launch needs before anything gets ordered:

  • A named primary target with a sized market behind it, roughly $7B of a $22.1B US category, and a stated reason for leaving education and occupation out of the segmentation.
  • A geographic launch order weighted to the warm regions, with the cold-region traveller handled as her own tier instead of being ignored.
  • A positioning statement built on fit and body diversity rather than volume, and a message platform to carry it.
  • The $2M promotional budget split across Instagram, Pinterest, TikTok, fashion press and targeted display, against a 5 to 7.5% conversion target.
  • A price and margin structure at $29.99 that still works once the planned discounts come off it.

I've put this next to an allocation workbook on purpose. They're the same job at different points in the calendar: work out who the product is for and where it should go, before the money is committed. An allocator just does it one size and one store at a time.

Built artifact · Excel / Google Sheets

Allocation & In-Stock Tracker

An allocation workbook rather than a forecast. It doesn't try to guess what will sell. It takes the stock that already exists and works out where it's sitting wrong and what should move.

One intimate apparel style, sizes 32A to 38DD, nine stores, twelve weeks. That comes to 2,160 rows of store by size by week. Four analysis tabs all read off that one data tab, and every figure on them is a live formula. Nothing is pasted in as a value.

  • Store performance. Sell-through %, weeks of supply and in-stock %, all built out of SUMIFS, AVERAGEIFS and COUNTIFS.
  • Summary grid. Store by week, driven by formulas rather than a pivot table, with red / amber / green banding against the weeks-of-supply range.
  • Transfer recommendations, which is really the point of the thing. It takes the store with the most to spare above the ceiling, matches it to the store with the biggest gap below the floor, sizes the move so neither one ends up outside the range afterwards, then writes out the reason in plain English.
  • Size curve analysis. Each store's sales mix by size against what it's actually holding by size, and the gap between the two.
  • Formula reference. Every calculated column, what it means and why an allocator would care.

Every threshold lives in a single named cell: the weeks-of-supply floor and ceiling, the rebalance target, the smallest quantity worth shipping, the review week. Change one and the whole workbook re-runs. It's built to survive the import into Google Sheets, so there are no pivot tables, no macros, no XLOOKUP and no merged cells.

On the sample data it calls 17 transfers moving 281 units. It also flags a store that looks fine on total cover, 12.3 weeks of supply and 90% in stock, while 56% of its units sit in sizes it doesn't sell. You'd never catch that from a total-units view.

Sample data throughout. I made it up to show the method, and none of it comes from any employer.
