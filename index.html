Case study — consumer segmentation & market sizing

WearPepper: the Hathor Collection

A swimwear line proposed for a direct-to-consumer intimates brand built around AA, A and B cups — and the segmentation and sizing work that had to happen before a dollar of the launch budget was committed.

Challenge

WearPepper is a real DTC intimates brand, founded in 2017 by Jaclyn Fu and Lia Winograd, built on a single unserved fit problem: bras cut for AA, A and B cups rather than scaled down from a C. It had proven that thesis in bras and athleisure and wanted to extend into swimwear.

Swimwear is where that fit problem is worst. A bikini top cut for a fuller bust gaps at the cup and drags on the strap, and much of the category solves it by adding volume the customer never asked for. The commercial question was not whether the product could be made. It was who it is for, where they are, and whether that group is big enough to launch against — answered before the $2M promotional budget was allocated.

My role

Sole author. I wrote both documents — the integrated marketing communications plan and the segmentation framework underneath it — including the segmentation, the geographic tiering, the market sizing, the competitive review, the positioning statement and the price and margin structure.

Academic capstone, York University, 2023. WearPepper is a real company; the Hathor Collection and everything below are my proposal for it, produced as coursework. It was not a client engagement, and the line was never produced.

Approach

Three tiers — primary, secondary, tertiary — each profiled on the same four dimensions, so the tiers could actually be compared rather than just described.

  • Geography tiered by climate, not by population. Swimwear demand follows warm weather, so the primary tier was weighted to the warm-weather regions of Canada and the US and the secondary tier became the cold-region customer who travels.
  • Sizing top-down, then narrowed. Published category figures for swimwear and beachwear, reduced to the share of women in the applicable range.
  • Only the primary tier was sized. The secondary and tertiary tiers are not targeted directly with their own marketing mix, so putting a dollar figure against them would have been a number invented to fill a cell.

The work

Three-tier segmentation framework, and the addressable market it sizes.

Primary

The Core Fit

Women who wear AA–B cups and cannot buy swimwear that fits them.

Geography
Warm-weather markets only. Canada Pacific coast, Prairie provinces, Central Canada US Southwest, Lower Midwest, Southeast, California, Texas
Demographics
Women, 18–50. Family life cycle: young single, married, family nests 1–3, empty nest 1. Lower-middle class and above. Education and occupation deliberately excluded as segmenting variables — they do not predict this need. ≈32% of women fall in the applicable range.
Lifestyle & values
Socially active with discretionary income. Treat what they wear to the beach as self-expression rather than as swim kit. Want a flattering fit, not added volume.
Buying behaviour
The need stated in their own words: a top that does not gap at the cup and does not slide on the strap. Bought through Instagram, the brand's own site, and stockists already carrying the bras. Decided on research and word of mouth, against fit and a clear point of difference. Peaks in summer.

Secondary

Globetrotters & First Fit

Cold-region travellers, and adolescents buying a first swimsuit that fits.

Geography
The inverse of the primary tier — cold regions, buying ahead of travel. Canada including the northern territories US Mountain frontier, Upper Midwest, Northeastern highlands First Fit national, Canada and the US
Demographics
Globetrotters — women 18–50, same life-cycle spread as the primary tier, lower-middle class and above, well paid and educated.
First Fit — 10–17. US: 13% of the adolescent population, 49% of them female. Canada: 15%.
Lifestyle & values
Globetrotters — discretionary income, socially active, swimwear as self-expression.
First Fit — spending follows a caregiver's income, not their own. The purchase carries the weight of a milestone.
Buying behaviour
Same product need and the same channels as the primary tier. The difference is timing: they buy against a trip, not against a season — which pulls demand outside the summer peak.

Tertiary

Turtle Doves

Men buying swimwear as a gift.

Geography
National across Canada, warm and cold regions alike — a gift does not follow the weather where the buyer lives.
Demographics
Men, 18–50. Young adults, married, family nests 1–3, empty nest 1. Lower-middle class and above. Education and occupation do not affect the buying decision.
Lifestyle & values
Discretionary income. The value sits with the person receiving it, not with the product — which changes what the messaging has to do.
Buying behaviour
Buys on the recipient's behalf, so fit is a risk rather than a benefit. Peaks at holidays and celebrations, not in summer — the only tier whose calendar runs opposite to the category.

Each tier is profiled on the same four dimensions so they can be compared, not just listed. The three calendars — summer, travel and gifting — are what turned this from a description of customers into a promotional calendar.

Gross margin is arithmetic on the cost lines stated in the plan, not a separate estimate. It is the number that has to survive the discount ladder built on top of it — 20% first order, 15% seasonal, 10% bundle, 10% referral, 10% wholesale.

Outcome

This was coursework. The collection was never produced, so there are no sales results and I am not going to invent any. What the work produced was the set of decisions a launch actually needs:

  • A named primary target with a sized addressable market — ≈$7B of a $22.1B US category — and a stated reason for excluding education and occupation from the segmentation.
  • A geographic launch order, weighted to warm-weather regions, with the cold-region traveller handled as a separate tier rather than ignored.
  • A positioning statement built on fit and body diversity rather than on volume, and a message platform to carry it.
  • A $2M promotional budget allocated across Instagram, Pinterest, TikTok, fashion press and targeted display, against a stated 5–7.5% conversion target.
  • A price and margin structure at $29.99 that holds up under the discount ladder planned on top of it.

The transferable part is not the marketing plan. It is that the work decides who a product is for and where it goes before anyone buys it — which is the same question an allocator answers one size and one store at a time, and the reason the workbook below exists.

Built artifact — Excel / Google Sheets

Allocation & In-Stock Tracker

An allocation workbook, not a forecast. It does not predict what will sell. It takes the inventory that already exists and answers two questions: where is it wrong, and what should move.

One intimate apparel style across a 32A–38DD size range, nine stores, twelve weeks — 2,160 rows of store × size × week. Four analysis tabs all read off that single data tab, and every figure on them is a live formula. Nothing is pasted as a value.

  • Store performance — sell-through %, weeks of supply, in-stock %, each built from SUMIFS, AVERAGEIFS and COUNTIFS.
  • Summary grid — store × week, formula-driven rather than a pivot table, with red / amber / green banding against the weeks-of-supply band.
  • Transfer recommendations — the centrepiece. It matches the store with the most to spare above the ceiling against the store with the biggest gap below the floor, sizes the move so neither store ends up outside the band, and writes the reason in plain English.
  • Size curve analysis — each store's sales mix by size against its inventory mix by size, and the variance between them.
  • Formula reference — every calculated column, what it means, and why an allocator cares.

Every threshold — the weeks-of-supply floor and ceiling, the rebalance target, the minimum shippable quantity, the review week — is a single named cell. Change one and the whole workbook re-runs. Built to import into Google Sheets intact: no pivot tables, no macros, no XLOOKUP, no merged cells.

On the sample data it calls 17 transfers moving 281 units, and flags a store that reads healthy on total cover — 12.3 weeks of supply, 90% in stock — while 56% of its units sit in sizes it does not sell. That is the failure a total-units view never shows you.

Illustrative sample data throughout — invented to demonstrate the method, not taken from any employer.
